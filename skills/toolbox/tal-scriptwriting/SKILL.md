# TAL — VIRAL SCRIPTWRITING SKILL

## Purpose

This skill defines how to write, rewrite, research, structure, and improve short-form scripts in Tal's preferred style.

Load this skill whenever the user asks for:

- Instagram Reel scripts
- TikTok scripts
- YouTube Shorts
- Talking-head scripts
- Voiceover scripts
- News/commentary scripts
- Storytelling scripts
- Founder/business content
- Travel or food content
- Viral educational content
- Hooks
- Script rewrites
- Turning an article, tweet, video, story, transcript, or idea into a script

The goal is not to sound like an AI writing a "viral script."
The goal is to sound like a smart person telling you something so interesting that you naturally keep listening.

## 0. THE ONE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST — give critical information to the person it belongs to

Before anything else: whenever a script contains information that is critical, or that belongs to a specific person — what they built, what they discovered, what they believe, what happened to them — that information gets said BY that person, on camera, in their own words. Not narrated about them by TAL or VO. This is the single most emphasized correction across every session with Tal: move the important stuff to the person, the way RedC Biotech does it (Ari says "Hi, I'm Ari," and the key facts about his own blood, his own accident, his own company come from him — Tal narrates around him, never in place of him). VO/TAL narration exists to carry setup, scale, transition, and pacing — not to explain the one fact that matters most when the actual person is standing right there to say it themselves. Check this on every script before anything else.

## 1. THE CORE PHILOSOPHY

The script must make the viewer feel:
"Wait… what?"
then: "Okay, tell me more."
then: "No way."
then: "Now I understand why this matters."

Retention comes from curiosity, information gaps, escalation, specificity, and storytelling. Not from fake hype.

Every sentence must earn the next sentence.

## 2. THE VOICE

Write like someone speaking naturally to a friend.

Not like: a journalist reading an article, a corporate marketer, a motivational speaker, ChatGPT, a documentary narrator trying too hard, a LinkedIn post, an academic essay.

The voice should feel: confident, conversational, sharp, curious, slightly provocative when appropriate, informed, fast-moving, simple, human.

The speaker understands the subject deeply but explains it simply. Never use complicated language just to sound intelligent. If a 15-year-old can understand the sentence without making the idea less accurate, use that sentence.

## 3. THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE

WRITE FOR THE EAR, NOT THE EYE.

The script will be spoken. Sentences should sound good out loud.

Prefer: "And this is where it gets interesting." over "This development is particularly noteworthy due to several underlying factors."

Prefer: "But there was one problem." over "However, the company faced a significant challenge."

Prefer: "Here's the crazy part." — only when the next fact genuinely deserves it.

Natural spoken rhythm is more important than perfect written grammar.

## 4. HOOK PHILOSOPHY

The first line has one job: Make stopping feel harder than continuing.

Do not waste the opening introducing the topic.

BAD: "Today I want to tell you about one of the most interesting restaurants in Tokyo."
BAD: "Did you know that there is a restaurant in Tokyo that..."
BAD: "Here are three things you need to know about..."

BETTER: "This restaurant has been fully booked for almost ten years."
BETTER: "McDonald's spent millions trying to solve a problem this tiny restaurant figured out by accident."
BETTER: "There's a restaurant in Tokyo where getting a table is almost impossible — and it only has eight seats."

The hook should contain one or more of: unexpected fact, contradiction, unanswered question, strong claim, recognizable person/company/place, money, scale, danger, conflict, transformation, hidden reason, unusual consequence, social proof, status, something the viewer thinks they understand but actually doesn't.

## 5. NEVER GIVE AWAY THE WHOLE STORY IN THE HOOK

Create an information gap.

BAD: "This restaurant became famous because Anthony Bourdain visited it in 2008." — no reason to continue.

BETTER: "This tiny restaurant was almost completely unknown. Then one customer walked through the door and changed the owner's life."

Now the viewer wants to know: Who? What happened? Why?

## 6. HOOKS SHOULD BE CONCRETE

Specificity beats generic hype.

BAD: "This company is absolutely insane." → BETTER: "This company loses money every time you buy its most popular product."
BAD: "He made a huge amount of money." → BETTER: "He turned $40,000 into a company worth more than $2 billion."
BAD: "The restaurant became extremely popular." → BETTER: "Within six months, the waiting list was three months long."

Numbers, names, places, timeframes, and real consequences make stories believable.

## 7. DO NOT USE FAKE VIRAL LANGUAGE

Avoid default AI hook phrases unless genuinely appropriate: "You won't believe…", "This is absolutely insane.", "This changes everything.", "Nobody is talking about this.", "Here's what they don't want you to know.", "This might be the craziest thing ever.", "Let that sink in.", "Read that again.", "Game changer.", "Buckle up.", "Imagine this…", "In today's fast-paced world…", "But here's the kicker."

The story should create excitement. The adjectives should not have to do the work.

## 8. THE DEFAULT SCRIPT STRUCTURE

Use this as a mental model, not a rigid template.

1. **Hook** — introduce the most compelling tension.
2. **Immediate context** — minimum information necessary to understand the story.
3. **Open a question** — introduce what the viewer now needs answered.
4. **Escalate** — each new piece of information should make the story stranger, bigger, more consequential, clearer, more impressive, more controversial, or more unexpected.
5. **Reveal** — deliver the information promised by the hook.
6. **Meaning** — explain why it matters.
7. **Exit** — end immediately after the payoff unless there is a strong reason for a CTA. Do not keep talking after the story is finished.

## 9. THE "BUT / SO" RULE

Stories move through cause and consequence.

Prefer: "He opened the restaurant in 2012. But almost nobody came. So he started giving meals away to local taxi drivers. And that worked. But it created another problem."

Instead of: "He opened the restaurant in 2012. The restaurant served Italian food. It had 30 seats. He also promoted it locally."

The second version is information. The first version is a story.

Connect beats using: but, so, except, until, because, which meant, then, instead, that's when.

## 10. CONSTANT FORWARD MOTION

Do not write paragraphs where nothing changes. Every 1–3 sentences should introduce new information, a consequence, a twist, a question, an escalation, or a reveal.

If three sentences merely explain the same idea, compress them.

## 11. ONE IDEA PER SENTENCE

BAD: "After launching the company in 2018, the founder, who previously worked at Google and had already built two startups, realized that the market was shifting toward..."

BETTER: "He launched the company in 2018. Before that, he worked at Google. And he had already built two startups. But this company was different."

Short does not mean childish. Short means easy to process.

## 12. USE LINE BREAKS AS PACING

Scripts should visually reflect spoken rhythm. Break into short lines, not giant paragraphs. This makes editing, delivery, captions, and pacing easier.

## 13. CREATE MINI OPEN LOOPS

The viewer should rarely feel completely caught up until the final payoff.

Examples: "But that wasn't actually the reason they bought the company." / "There was one problem." / "And this is where the story gets weird." / "Except he had already made one massive mistake." / "What happened next turned the restaurant into a global phenomenon."

Use these naturally. Do not insert an artificial cliffhanger every ten seconds.

## 14. DELAY INFORMATION — BUT DON'T ANNOY THE VIEWER

Curiosity is useful. Dragging out obvious information is annoying. Reveal it when the reveal creates the most impact. The viewer must feel rewarded, not manipulated.

## 15. CONTEXT MUST BE COMPRESSED

Only explain context required to understand what happens next. Ask: "If I delete this sentence, does the viewer still understand the story?" If yes, strongly consider deleting it.

## 16. START CLOSE TO THE INTERESTING PART

Do not begin with unnecessary chronology. Start at the moment of tension and backfill history later, unless the founding/origin is essential to the payoff.

## 17. PEOPLE > ABSTRACT CONCEPTS

Instead of: "The company experienced difficulty entering the market." Write: "For two years, the founders couldn't convince a single major retailer to sell it."

Show the consequence through people.

## 18. MAKE SCALE UNDERSTANDABLE

Large numbers alone often mean nothing.

BAD: "The company processed 14 billion transactions." BETTER: "That's more than 400 transactions every second for an entire year."

Use comparisons only when they genuinely make the scale easier to understand.

## 19. CONTRAST IS ONE OF THE BEST STORYTELLING TOOLS

Look for: before vs. after, expected vs. actual, rich vs. broke, huge company vs. tiny competitor, popular perception vs. reality, simple object vs. enormous business, failure vs. success.

Example: "Today it's worth billions. But when he started it, he couldn't convince his own friends to use it."

Contrast creates tension without needing fake drama.

## 20. FIND THE REAL STORY

When given an article or topic, don't simply summarize it. Ask: "What is actually interesting here?"

The most interesting story may be: the founder, the hidden business model, the failure before success, the absurd economics, the person behind it, the unexpected reason something happened, the mistake, the controversy, the loophole, the consequence, the historical connection, what almost happened instead.

The title of the source does not determine the script angle.

## 21. RESEARCH BEFORE WRITING WHEN FACTS MATTER

When a script involves current events, companies, famous people, money, politics, history, scientific claims, statistics, lawsuits, acquisitions, valuations, quotes, or dates — verify important facts before confidently using them.

Prefer: 1) primary sources, 2) official documentation, 3) reputable reporting, 4) strong secondary sources.

Avoid building an entire hook around an unverified viral claim. If sources disagree, write around the uncertainty rather than pretending certainty exists.

## 22. FACTS SHOULD SUPPORT THE STORY

Research is ammunition. It is not the script. Do not dump every interesting fact found. Choose facts based on: "Does this make the story better?" A researched script may use only 10% of the research. That is fine.

## 23. NEVER FABRICATE DETAILS TO MAKE THE STORY BETTER

Do not invent quotes, conversations, emotions, intentions, exact numbers, dramatic scenes, timelines, or causal relationships.

If someone's motivation is unknown, don't write "He knew this would be his last chance." unless there is evidence. Instead: "And at that point, the company was running out of options."

Facts can already be dramatic.

## 24. WRITING ABOUT CONTROVERSY

Do not flatten complicated subjects into fake certainty. Separate: what definitely happened, what someone alleges, what critics argue, what supporters argue, what is interpretation. But don't bury the script in legal language — make the distinction naturally.

Example: "Critics say the policy does X. The government says the opposite — that it was created to Y. The actual rule does Z."

## 25. STORY FIRST, OPINION SECOND

When discussing news or controversial events, establish what happened before telling the audience what to think about it. A stronger script often lets the facts create the reaction.

## 26. THE PAYOFF MUST MATCH THE HOOK

If the opening promises a reveal, the script must actually deliver it. Do not hook one story and deliver another. Before finalizing, ask: "Did I answer the question created by line one?" If not, the script is broken.

## 27. ENDINGS

Most scripts should end on the strongest final piece of information. Good ending types:

- **The Reveal** — "And that's why the company intentionally loses money on every one it sells."
- **The Consequence** — "Which means the tiny decision they made in 1997 still controls how billions of people use the internet today."
- **The Irony** — "So the product everyone thought would destroy the company ended up saving it."
- **The Full Circle** — reconnect the ending to the hook.
- **A genuine question** — only when discussion is actually interesting.

## 28. DO NOT FORCE A CTA

Do not automatically end with "Follow for more." / "Like and share." / "What do you think?" / "Comment below." Use a CTA only when requested or strategically useful. A strong ending is often better for retention than an obvious engagement request.

## 29. WORD ECONOMY

Remove filler: basically, essentially, in order to, the fact that, it is important to note, interestingly enough, as you may know, as previously mentioned, when it comes to, in today's world, at the end of the day, needless to say.

Say the thing.

## 30. NO AI SLOP

Never default to: "It's not just X — it's Y." / "But here's the thing." / "And the best part?" / "This isn't just about X." / "It's a reminder that..." / "In a world where..." / "That's the power of..." / "This raises an important question..." / "The answer might surprise you."

Occasionally one can work. Repeated use makes the script sound generated.

## 31. AVOID OVERLY CINEMATIC WRITING

Don't write: "Little did he know, this single decision would forever alter the course of his life." — unless the requested style genuinely calls for cinematic narration.

Prefer: "That decision ended up changing his life." Even better, show exactly how.

## 32. DON'T OVEREXPLAIN

Trust the viewer.

BAD: "The reason this mattered was because having more users made the company more valuable, which is known as a network effect."
BETTER: "Every new user made the product more useful to everyone already on it." Then continue the story.

## 33. DON'T SOUND LIKE A TEACHER

Avoid "First, let's understand what X means." Instead, explain it inside the story: "Every time someone joined, their friends had another reason to join too."

## 34. TONE ADJUSTMENT BY SUBJECT

- **Business** — money, incentives, business model, clever distribution, failures, competitive advantage, unexpected economics.
- **Founder stories** — decisions, rejection, risk, mistakes, pivots, turning points.
- **Travel / Food** — why the place is special, visual details, story, cultural context, unusual food, price, experience, whether it is actually worth visiting. Avoid generic "This hidden gem is a must visit."
- **News** — start with what changed, then why, why it matters, what happens next.
- **History** — find the human story or strange connection, not a textbook timeline.

## 35. VISUAL WRITING

When appropriate, write sentences that naturally suggest footage. "He started by selling shoes out of the trunk of his car." gives the editor a visual. Scripts should create images in the viewer's head.

## 36. SCRIPT LENGTH

Unless otherwise requested:

- **15–25 seconds** — ~45–75 words. One simple idea.
- **30–45 seconds** — ~80–130 words. One story with a fast payoff.
- **45–60 seconds** — ~120–170 words. Setup + escalation + reveal.
- **60–90 seconds** — ~160–240 words. Use only when the story actually deserves the extra context.

Do not make a script longer simply because more information exists.

## 37. THE COMPRESSION TEST

After the first draft: try removing 20%. If nothing important disappears, the original was too long. Then ask: Can the first interesting moment happen earlier? Can two sentences become one? Can context move later? Can a generic sentence become a specific fact?

## 38. HOOK GENERATION PROCESS

Before writing, internally generate several angles:

- **Outcome hook** — "This teenager built a company worth $4 billion."
- **Contradiction hook** — "The world's most successful ___ does the exact opposite of everyone else."
- **Mystery hook** — "There's a reason ___ refuses to ___."
- **Failure hook** — "Before ___ became successful, it nearly died because of one decision."
- **Money hook** — "This $5 product quietly generates more money than ___."
- **Hidden mechanism** — "Every time you do ___, this company makes money — even if you never pay them."
- **Person hook** — "In 1998, one man made a decision that still affects ___ today."

Select the angle with the strongest combination of curiosity, credibility, emotional stakes, simplicity, and payoff. Do not simply choose the loudest one.

## 39. SCRIPT REWRITE MODE

When the user supplies a script, preserve the underlying idea unless asked to change it. Improve: hook, clarity, pacing, spoken language, curiosity, structure, compression, ending. Do not unnecessarily replace the user's personality with generic copywriting. If the original sentence is already strong, leave it alone.

## 40. USER'S ROUGH LANGUAGE IS NOT A PROBLEM

The user may explain ideas through fragments, voice transcription, spelling mistakes, repeated thoughts, unfinished sentences, examples. Extract the intended idea. Do not imitate transcription errors in the finished script. Preserve the energy and thought, not the typos.

## 41. DO NOT CHANGE THE CENTRAL IDEA WITHOUT A REASON

If the user has a specific angle, do not replace it with the angle you personally prefer. Improve the execution first. If another angle is dramatically stronger, mention it separately.

## 42. KEEP IMPORTANT USER LINES

If the user writes a line that is original, funny, sharp, memorable, or authentic — keep it whenever possible. Polish around it rather than sanitizing it.

## 43. LANGUAGE

Write in whatever language the user requests.

For Hebrew: write conversational Israeli Hebrew, avoid translated-English syntax, avoid overly formal Hebrew, use language people genuinely say aloud, preserve English brand/product names naturally when appropriate.

For English: use natural spoken English, contractions encouraged when natural, do not sound academic.

Translation should preserve the effect, not blindly preserve sentence structure.

## 44. NEVER MAKE THE CREATOR SOUND DUMBER TO SOUND CASUAL

Simple language ≠ dumb language. The speaker should feel intelligent precisely because complicated things become easy to understand.

## 45. EMOTION THROUGH FACTS

Instead of "It was devastating." use "He had $11 left in the company account."
Instead of "The restaurant became unbelievably successful." use "People started flying in from other countries just to eat there."

Specific consequences create emotion better than adjectives.

## 46. RHYTHM

Mix sentence lengths. Example: "They said no. Again. And again. By the time the 27th investor rejected him, he was almost out of money. Then investor number 28 said yes."

Rhythm creates emphasis. Do not make every sentence artificially tiny.

## 47. EMPHASIS

Save short standalone lines for important moments: "There was only one problem." / "He lied." / "It worked." If every sentence is dramatic, none of them are.

## 48. NAMES AND DETAILS

Introduce only as many names as the audience can remember. Reduce cognitive load — e.g. "Five people started the company. But one of them — Marc — made the decision that changed everything," rather than listing five names.

## 49. CHRONOLOGY

Chronological order is optional. Often stronger: 1) surprising present-day outcome, 2) jump backwards, 3) explain how it happened, 4) return to outcome with new meaning.

Example: "Today, this painting is worth $100 million. But for decades, almost nobody wanted it."

## 50. WHAT MAKES A STORY VIRAL

Do not chase virality mechanically. Strong short-form stories usually contain at least two of: familiarity, surprise, status, money, conflict, emotion, usefulness, novelty, absurdity, transformation, controversy, strong visual potential, identity, cultural relevance.

Identify which ingredients exist naturally. Do not manufacture ones that don't.

## 51. WHAT TO DO WITH BORING SOURCE MATERIAL

If the source is boring, don't write a boring summary. Search for: origin story, financial implication, historical precedent, person affected, surprising number, comparison, consequence, disagreement, contradiction. If none exists, simplify the topic instead of faking drama.

## 52. THE "WHY SHOULD I CARE?" TEST

At every major section, the importance should become obvious through the story — not by literally writing "Here's why you should care."

## 53. THE FIRST 5 SECONDS

Within roughly five seconds, the viewer should understand: what world we are in, what is unusual, what question they are waiting to have answered. Do not spend the first five seconds on setup.

## 54. NO PREAMBLE INSIDE THE SCRIPT

Never start: "Okay guys...", "So today...", "I wanted to talk about...", "I recently came across...", "You guys have been asking..." — unless there is a specific creative reason. Start with the story.

## 55. AVOID LISTICLE WRITING UNLESS REQUESTED

Default: story > list. Lists are useful when the content itself is genuinely a list.

## 56. SCRIPT SHOULD HAVE A THESIS

After researching, be able to summarize the script privately in one sentence: "This is a story about ___." If unclear, the script probably contains too many ideas.

## 57. EDITING FOR RETENTION

On the second pass, inspect every sentence: Does it create curiosity? Keep. Does it answer necessary curiosity? Keep. Does it create context required for the next beat? Keep. Is it merely interesting trivia? Probably cut. Does it repeat something we understand? Cut. Is the sentence generic? Make it specific.

## 58. DO NOT CLICKBAIT PAST THE FACTS

The hook can frame information dramatically. It cannot make the underlying event materially different from reality. Curiosity must survive fact-checking.

## 59. SOURCING AND QUOTES

When quoting someone, use their real words when verified. Otherwise paraphrase. Never write fake dialogue unless there is a reliable source showing that exchange happened.

## 60. FINAL SCRIPT QUALITY CHECK

Before delivering a script, silently score it against: Hook, Curiosity, Clarity, Pacing, Specificity, Human voice, Payoff, Compression, Accuracy, AI smell. If any category is weak, revise before delivering.

## 61. OUTPUT FORMAT

Unless the user asks otherwise, output a clean script. Do not clutter it with unnecessary explanations. Use short paragraphs / line breaks corresponding roughly to spoken beats. Do not include labels like HOOK or SCRIPT if the user wants something directly usable for teleprompter.

## 62. WHEN ASKED FOR MULTIPLE HOOKS

Do not write ten tiny variations of the same sentence. Each hook should use a genuinely different psychological angle (mystery, money, contradiction, person, consequence, controversy, scale). Then choose the strongest default.

## 63. WHEN THE USER SAYS "MAKE IT BETTER"

Do not simply add dramatic adjectives. Usually improve by: moving the strongest fact earlier, deleting context, making claims more specific, increasing contrast, introducing conflict, shortening sentences, improving the payoff, creating a stronger information gap.

Better ≠ louder. Better = harder to stop watching.

## 64. WHEN THE USER SAYS "MAKE IT MORE VIRAL"

Increase: curiosity, clarity, stakes, novelty, emotional contrast, speed to value, hook strength, visual potential.

Do not automatically increase: exaggeration, caps, fake drama, clickbait, buzzwords.

## 65. WHEN THE USER GIVES AN EXAMPLE SCRIPT

Treat examples as style training. Identify: average sentence length, vocabulary, pacing, hook mechanics, humor, transitions, information density, level of opinion, type of ending, use of rhetorical questions, amount of context, narrative structure. Reproduce the underlying writing system — don't merely copy phrases.

## 66. STYLE PRECEDENCE

When instructions conflict, follow this priority:

1. Explicit instruction in the current request
2. Specific example/reference supplied by the user
3. This scriptwriting skill
4. Generic copywriting conventions

The user's desired voice always outranks generic "best practices."

## 67. THE TAL TEST

The final script should feel like: "I just learned something fascinating from a smart friend." Not: "A content marketer is trying to keep me watching." The viewer should notice the story. They should not notice the retention techniques.

## 68. FINAL PRINCIPLE

The best script does not feel written. It feels discovered.

Find the most interesting truth in the subject. Put it first. Make the viewer need the next sentence. Remove everything that gets in the way. And stop when the story is over.

## Production Blueprint (Tal Daily engine)

This is the concrete, filming-ready production layer. It governs formatting and structure for any script produced by this skill unless the user explicitly asks for a plain teleprompter script (see Rule 61).

### Mandatory pre-writing rules

- **100% fact verification.** Never invent or fabricate statistics, dates, percentages, names, or outcomes. Every claim must be backed by provided source data or verified via web search.
- **No marketing clichés.** Strictly eliminate corporate boilerplate — "beacon of hope," "revolutionizing the landscape," "unwavering dedication," "at its core."
- **Micro-line formatting.** Format dialogue into short, rhythmic 1-to-7-word lines for teleprompter reading, fast voiceovers, and high-retention subtitles.
- **Timing & pacing calibration.** Target 90–180 seconds (~1.5–3 min), running 130–150 words/min (~200–220 words for 90s; ~400–450 words for 3 min).
- **No standalone question-and-answer traps.** Avoid filler rhetorical questions ("But why?", "How?", "What happened next?"). Anchor questions exclusively in dynamic on-camera dialogue — never as unfilmed narration filler. Keep the script educational, active, momentum-driven.

### Required production labels

Every generated script must contain zero unlabeled prose. Every line is tagged with one of:

- `TAL ON CAMERA` (or designated host, e.g. `MONTANA ON CAMERA`) — spoken direct-to-lens dialogue by the host.
- `SUBJECT ON CAMERA` — spoken lines by the featured creator, founder, patient, or student.
- `VOICEOVER` — spoken narration delivered over B-roll.
- `CONVERSATION` — dynamic, natural dialogue handoffs between host and subject.
- `CAMERA SHOT` — concrete visual cues, physical actions, reveals, prop interactions, camera moves, scene transitions.
- `GROUP ON CAMERA` — high-energy group unison shout moments (e.g. "BOYS TOWN JERUSALEM!", "TABI!").

### The 6-step narrative arc

`[Visual Hook] → [Rapid Handoff] → [Problem & Origin] → [Action Mechanism] → [Concrete Proof] → [Reframed Outro]`

1. **The Visual & Paradox Hook (0–5s)** — start directly on camera with an unexpected physical prop, location, or visual contradiction before providing background explanation. Examples: holding a satellite beacon, crumpling a dollar bill, standing next to a bioreactor, opening on an extreme traffic map.
2. **Rapid Opening Handoffs (5–15s)** — transition rapidly between Host, Subject ("Hi, I'm [Name], and I built [X]!"), and visual action before introducing voiceover.
3. **Problem & Origin (the contrast engine)** — frame the stakes through stark, simple contrasts (the world before vs. the breakdown/crisis).
4. **Action-Driven Mechanism** — demystify complex technology, biology, or social systems using physical actions (pressing an SOS button, sorting sand and gravel, growing cells in bioreactors, diverting traffic corridor lights) rather than technical jargon.
5. **Concrete Proof Points** — deploy verified, high-impact numbers late in the story (100M unit deficit, 30,000 stores, 75% traffic cut, 99.9% saved).
6. **The Reframed Outro** — loop back to reframe the opening visual/prop/paradox into an earned human truth. Close with the signature sign-off: "That's another story from [Israel/Jerusalem] the headlines don't show you. Follow for more!"

### The 5 master script categories

Identify the core narrative engine of the topic and apply the matching category:

1. **Surprising Object Explainer** — transforms an ordinary or curious physical item into a life-saving or breakthrough symbol (Purple Pinky, Sand Dams, Permethrin, Garmin SOS Device, Blood Vial).
2. **Unexpected Location Story** — uses the setting itself as the hook and contrast before diving into the solution (doctor inside a vegetable market, tech campus in historic Jerusalem, mosque bridge-building).
3. **Founder & Startup Story** — bold claim/prop → humble origins → market failure/crisis → the physical pivot → scale & proof (Zendrop Hoverboard, Mobility Insight FIFA traffic, Prayut Coffee).
4. **Emotional Transformation Story** — binds personal tragedy, physical loss, or family promises directly to an enduring social mission or invention (A Father's Promise, Memeza Alarm, RedC Biotech paragliding crash).
5. **Shock-and-Explain Video** — captures immediate curiosity via a startling or counter-intuitive claim, immediately pivoting into a clear, accessible educational breakdown (Blood Soup, Mosquitoes vs. Sharks, Country Drinking the Ocean).

## The distilled template (confirmed recurring devices — use these by default)

This is the synthesis of everything the 23+ studied scripts below have in common. Where the 68 rules are theory and the studied-scripts log is raw evidence, this section is the working template: the specific, repeatable moves confirmed across multiple real scripts, ready to reach for by default when writing something new — not just referenced when convenient.

**Hook devices (open with one of these):**
- **Location/prop before label** — ground an abstract or invisible subject in something physical and point at it before naming what it is ("This is where AI happens... it's called data centers"; "the lake where Jesus walked on water... this is the Sea of Galilee").
- **Chorus/echo hook** — two or three real people stating the same identity fact or intro line back-to-back ("I'm a doctor." / "I am a doctor." / "And I'm a doctor."; "I am Jewish." / "I am Muslim."). Works for any two/three-subject framing — profession, religion, family.
- **Withheld-name mystery** — state a superlative fact and ask the viewer directly, or dare them, to guess ("This country drinks the ocean... what is this country?"). Reveal delayed to the payoff, not given away early.
- **First-person prop-on-self** — the object's origin is something that just happened to the host's own body, not a thing shown to them ("They took my blood, and five minutes later turned it into this").
- **Sensory-checklist-then-flip** — a 3-beat list of expected sensory facts, then one clause that reverses it ("This looks like plastic. It feels like plastic. It works like plastic. But watch what happens...").
- **Effort-as-proof** — state the travel/access effort taken to get the story before showing anything ("I flew all the way to London to get...").

**Escalation devices (use through the middle):**
- ~~Two/three-voice echo~~ — **RETIRED.** This device is confirmed across 4+ studied scripts in the corpus (#10, #12, #15, #20), but Tal explicitly said he does not like it and it should never be used going forward: "Take off the echo, I don't like that, never do that." Overrides the corpus pattern — do not use this device in any new script, even though it appears in prior examples. Leave the studied-script entries themselves unchanged (they're historical record of what worked before), but do not reproduce this specific device when writing something new.
- **Bystander/reaction soundbites** — real, unscripted-sounding astonishment from someone other than the main subject, dropped in right after the hook, carrying zero new information but doing pure excitement work ("Wow, cool." "What? That's crazy.").
- **Elimination-logic problem framing** — walk through why the obvious solutions don't work before revealing the one that does (no wind → no wind farm; no flat land → no normal solar → floating solar).
- **Anaphora contrast** — 2–3 back-to-back parallel-structure before/after pairs against a shared known baseline ("Doctors check for one thing. Here, dozens." / "...thousands of dollars. Here, $372." / "...wait for hours. Here, instantly.").
- **Cumulative small-fact list** — build the "insane" feeling through volume of small concrete facts rather than one big stat (a chain of family members who all became doctors; a sequence of small verified scenes proving coexistence).
- **Mid-script humor/CONVERSATION beat** — a short, real, unscripted-feeling back-and-forth dropped in purely for levity, deliberately breaking the information momentum (the fish-soup exchange; the "for both of us? — no, just you" gravity joke).
- **Honest uncertainty tagging** — flag a shaky-but-plausible claim as such instead of stating it as fact ("According to AI, that's one of the highest in the world").

**Proof devices:**
- Proof can land early (inside/right after the hook) or late (as its own beat near the end) — both are attested; default to early placement when the stat itself is part of what makes the hook land (price/scale contrasts), late placement when the story needs to earn it first.
- Pair one engineering/scale number with one human/social number back to back (350,000 panels + 14 football fields, alongside 1,400 jobs + 50,000 homes powered).
- Regulatory/institutional credibility (FDA-cleared, Medicare-reimbursed, government approval) is a valid proof type distinct from raw scale stats — use it for medical/health subjects specifically.
- Real quotes should carry feeling sometimes, not just facts — a Vlog-style line can exist purely for emotional color ("my feeling was like I have a newborn child").

**Outro devices (close with one of these):**
- **Full-circle prop/image callback** — return to and re-affirm the exact hook image or claim ("This is not science fiction. It's real."; "Where Jesus walked on water will continue to live forever.").
- **Aphorism/maxim close** — generalize the specific story into a portable one-line truth ("plastic can either pollute or educate..."; "you don't beg for help, you don't wait for rain — you invent").
- **Negation-then-truth close** — state what the subject/invention is NOT before saying what it IS, closing an assumption gap the viewer would otherwise have (confirmed twice for scientist/inventor profiles specifically: "isn't artificial skin... isn't someone else's tissue... it was flowing through my veins"; "isn't trying to get us to stop using packaging... she's trying to change what happens after").
- **Documentary sign-off** — a plain stated conclusion after an explicit time/effort marker, appropriate for multi-day or access-journalism pieces rather than single-scene explainers ("After five days of touring the country... we are leaving Azerbaijan excited.").
- **Loop ending** — for single-topic tier-2 explainers only, the final line is written to loop back into the opening line ("...(loop)").
- **Deliberate repetition-as-rhetoric** — sparingly, repetition itself can be the closing device even where it breaks normal word-economy rules ("win-win-win-win-win-win") — use only when the moment has earned that much energy, not by default.

**Format modes (pick based on the ask):**
1. Full Tal Daily production blueprint (formal labels, 6-step arc, 90–180s) — for a fully produced multi-scene shoot.
2. Lean tier-2 explainer (title-as-hook, "Let me explain" bridge, Vlog-quote inserts, loop ending) — for a single-topic explainer or short founder story.
3. Dual-host investigative dialogue — mostly sustained real back-and-forth between two hosts/subjects rather than VOICEOVER-plus-quotes, for multi-day travel/culture investigations.
4. Access-journalism/on-site visit — Tal touring a real place or company, ending on documentary sign-off rather than callback or aphorism.

## Priority reference tier — examples #10–28 (Dead Sea physics through NeuroPod)

Tal flagged this stretch of the studied corpus as the best of the set — the primary reference tier for wording and pacing, ranked above the leaner tier-2 explainers (#1–9). When writing anything new, lean on these first. What sets them apart, synthesized across all nineteen:

**Wording traits:**
- Real, credentialed subjects introduced in one dense line ("I'm a chemical engineer, and I used to be a rocket scientist") — the juxtaposition itself does the work, no elaboration needed.
- Jargon gets an anatomical/physical metaphor instead of a definition ("one of the most important systems you have... called the vagus nerve") — never left to stand alone unglossed.
- Sentences stay short and declarative even when the underlying idea is technical — complexity lives in what's being said, not in sentence length.
- A found/quoted real line does emotional work a written line couldn't ("my feeling was like I have a newborn child") — Tal reaches for the subject's actual words over paraphrase whenever one exists.
- Closes favor a reframe or an honest reversal (negation-then-truth: "isn't artificial skin... it was flowing through my veins") over a generic wrap-up line.

**Pacing traits:**
- The two/three-voice echo (a short line, repeated almost verbatim by a second voice — "That's a lot." / "That's a lot.") recurs across at least four of these scripts. It's a deliberate, load-bearing tic, not incidental — use it.
- Proof numbers land early when the number IS the hook ($10,000 vs. $372 inside the first three lines of #22), late when the story needs to earn them first (#25's 3.5 million lives saved, held for the close).
- A mid-script humor or banter beat (two people, one unscripted-feeling exchange) deliberately breaks pure information momentum roughly once per script — never more than once.
- Escalation frequently runs on elimination logic (no wind → no wind farm; no flat land → no solar) rather than a single obstacle — walks the viewer through why the obvious answers fail before revealing the one that works.
- Endings rarely loop back to the hook's exact image the way the tier-2 explainers do; instead they widen the frame (personal → national scale, one fact → a portable maxim) or land on a reframed value statement.

Before writing a new script, re-read the 2–3 closest-matching entries in this tier by subject type (deep-tech/founder → #21, #22, #23, #24, #28; person-driven/emotional → #13, #25; unexpected-location → #14, #19; scale/explainer → #10, #18, #20) rather than defaulting straight to the rules or the tier-2 examples.

### 34. "The only city in America started by a woman" (Julia Tuttle, Miami) — Historical Story / Nas-Daily One-Minute Format
Subject: how Julia Tuttle convinced a railroad tycoon to extend his line to an empty swamp by mailing him oranges during the Great Freeze. Tal flagged this one as a script he really likes — study it for compression above all else.

> This is the only city in America started by a woman. And she did it with an orange. This is the story of Julia Tuttle. 130 years ago, Julia came to Miami. It was empty. No residents, no skyscrapers, nothing. But she saw potential. She bought 640 acres of land near the Miami River, and she waited for the winter. The entire East Coast froze. In 1896, Miami officially became a city, and Julia died two years later. This is her tomb. She never saw the skyscrapers. She never saw millions of people moving here. But look around you. Structural notes: the leanest script in the corpus — roughly 200 spoken words for a full 60 seconds, with no beat that exists only to inform. **New device — the scale-gap hook (two stacked claims where the second undercuts the first):** "This is the only city in America started by a woman" (scale, superlative, place) immediately followed by "And she did it with an orange" (an absurdly small object). The gap between a city and a piece of fruit *is* the retention engine; the viewer cannot leave until the gap closes. Distinct from the standard paradox hook because neither line is surprising alone — the surprise lives in the distance between them.

**New device — the unexplained action as an open loop.** "She bought 640 acres of land near the Miami River, and she waited for the winter." No tease, no "here's the crazy part," no announced mystery. It simply states an action that makes no sense yet, then moves on; the freeze detonates it four lines later. This is a stronger, quieter version of Rule 13 (mini open loops) — **do not announce the loop, just do something unexplained and keep going.** Worth reaching for over the corpus's more common "but the craziest thing about this place…" pattern-interrupt whenever the story contains a real unexplained action.

Perfect but/so causality (Rule 9) with no broken link across eleven beats: empty → *but* she saw potential → *so* she bought land → *and waited* → the coast froze → *except* Miami → *so* she mailed oranges → he understood → *so* he built the railroad → *so* Miami became a city → *but* she died two years later. Every beat is caused by the one before it.

**"This is her tomb."** Three words that give a 130-year-old story a present tense and a place to stand — the same job the warehouse does in the Aryeh script and the robotics lab does in Boys Town. Confirms that **any historical subject needs one physical, visitable anchor** or it stays an audio essay.

Close runs negation-then-truth (the #21/#23 outro family) into a full-circle object callback: "She never saw the skyscrapers. She never saw millions of people moving here. But look around you. This entire city exists because one woman mailed a few oranges." Note that it ends on the *small* object, not the big city — the callback lands on the orange, which is why it pays off rather than just concluding.

Accuracy notes for reuse: the tycoon is Henry Flagler (Standard Oil co-founder, not a "founder" of anything in Miami) — name him or say "the tycoon," since "the founder understood" reads as a different person. Historians treat the mailed-orange-blossoms detail as embellished-but-standard in the official Miami telling, so if this ever gets rewritten for broadcast, frame it as the story Miami tells rather than as a documented fact. Verified: Tuttle bought 640 acres, the Great Freeze was 1894–95, Miami incorporated July 1896, Tuttle died September 1898 (two years later), and she is widely credited as the only woman to found a major American city.

Format note: ends on the Nas-Daily sign-off ("That's one minute. See you tomorrow.") rather than Tal's own series tag — kept verbatim here as the source example, but swap in Tal's own close when adapting this structure.

**NEW STANDING CHECK — an opinion is not a hook; a fact is.** Tal's first draft opened on "I hate social media. I know that's ironic, since I'm posting this on social media right now." **Tal's own three-beat escalation is the best writing in the script**, and it came from him: "I had to hide my religion. / I even had to change my name. / I had to lie about where my mother was born." **NEW DEVICE — the personal proof of the thesis.** "Because for two years strangers were kind to me without ever knowing who I was. **That's how I know** love is more powerful than hate." The three words "that's how I know" convert a poster slogan into a conclusion the speaker earned. A thesis stated alone is a platitude; a thesis presented as the finding of a lived experiment is an argument. Reach for this whenever a script has to land an abstract value.

**CTA evolution across drafts, worth keeping as a record:** "Do you have an interesting story? Join us." (two questions and a shrug) → "Comment the word SOCIAL" (trackable but meaningless) → "Are you a creator who wants to use your platform to spread kindness?" (well targeted but excludes most viewers) → **"Do you believe that too? **Repetition audit on abstractions.** A draft ran "we're all human beings / we're in this world together / we're stronger together / love is more powerful than hate" back to back — four statements of one idea, which cancel each other out. Notes: **the deepest collaboration in the library — Tal wrote four full drafts himself and the final script is more his than mine.** Almost every strong device below came from one of his versions. The lesson is procedural: when Tal pastes an alternative draft and says "maybe combine both," the correct response is to name the two or three things his version does better *before* merging, and to cut whatever of mine they replace. Twice here a beat I was attached to had to die because his did the same job better.

**NEW DEVICE — plant the lie in the subject's own words, let him disprove it himself, never explain it.** Tal's find, and the best single thing in the script. DOR early: *"I was even told they had three legs."* DOR on the bus: *"I was looking for the Jews with three legs. But I couldn't find them."* Two lines, ninety seconds apart, same speaker, and the entire thesis of the video is made without a narrator saying one word about prejudice. **This is now the default way to handle any script where someone was taught something false about a group of people.** It beat the version I had built (soldiers giving him candy, his father saying it was poison, VO: "it wasn't poison") — and the candy had to be *cut*, because "they said poison / it wasn't poison" and "they said three legs / I couldn't find them" are the same rhetorical move twice in ninety seconds. **A repeat, not an echo: same shape, close together, the second one weakens the first.**

**NEW DEVICE — the two-voice escalation.** Also Tal's. VO states the category flatly: *"he was taught to hate Jews."* Then the subject escalates it in his own voice: *"I was taught to kill a Jew."* Hate → kill, across a voice change. It lands far harder than either voice saying both, because narration establishes the fact as ordinary and the subject's voice makes it personal and worse. Same family as the Aryeh "thousands of families, thank God" → "twelve thousand families" seam, but used for moral weight instead of precision. **Reach for this whenever the subject has a more extreme version of something a narrator can state plainly.**

**The missing link that made the bus plausible:** *"But he had never actually met a Jew."* / DOR: *"Not one."* Without it, "three legs" is a curiosity and the bus is a stunt. With it, both are inevitable — of course a child believes anything about people he has never seen, and of course the only way to find out is to go and look. Two words from the subject are the whole payload; the VO does the setup. **When an audience has to accept that someone believed something absurd, the fix is almost never more explanation — it is establishing that they had no way to check.**

**Sinwar placed immediately after "I had never met a Jew."** He had never met a single Jew, and he lived next door to the man who would organise the murder of 1,200 of them. Nothing connects the two lines out loud. The claim stays entirely in Dor's mouth — *"My neighbour was Yahya Sinwar. The guy who planned October 7th."* — which attributes it by construction. Tal's instruction was explicit that Mohammed Deif is **not** to be mentioned.

**The father arc — Tal's find, and the emotional spine.** A draft of mine had Nissim as a kind stranger who brought food. Tal rewrote it as a question and an answer: DOR: *"He asked me: 'Where is your father?' And I told him, 'I don't have a father.'"* … *"For me, he became my father."* Then he corrected the fact that made it land — and the correction reversed it: **Dor ran away from his father.** An interim draft had the father abandoning him at the building site; Tal's correction is that the boy escaped the man who, at seven, had told him killing a Jew was the greatest thing he could do. So "I don't have a father" is a *choice*, not a loss, and the video is a boy who runs from one father in Gaza and finds another in Israel — in a man he was raised to hate. The line that carries it is Tal's: **"And Dor ran. Not from Israel. From his father."** Note how much weaker the abandonment version was: being left is something done to you, and this script is only ever about what he chose. **The theme is never stated anywhere in the script.** Placing the abandonment *before* Nissim means the viewer already knows the answer to Nissim's question and has to watch the kid say it out loud.

**Delay the reason, not the fact.** When Dor is released from prison the script says only that a month later he was living on the street. *Why* — "the family he was born into called him a disgrace" — is held back forty seconds to the final block, where it is one half of a comparison instead of just another wound. Earlier drafts said "disgrace" in both places, which is the same-fact-twice error logged on Julius and Boys Town. **Moving the second instance far enough away and changing what it means converts a repeat into an echo.**

**Ending on the subject's aphorism, with nothing after it.** DOR: *"You cannot choose where you are born. But you can choose who you become."* Tal's line, from his own draft. His draft then followed it with "And this is Dor's story," which had to go: **never put a tag line after the strongest line in the script.** The house series sign-off ("another story from Israel the headlines don't show you") was moved to the outro card rather than spoken — the first script in the library to do that. Compare Julius, where the tag *does* follow the subject's close, because "everything is possible" is a softer landing than this one.

**NEW STANDING CHECK — never define a word the ending already embodies.** The script closed on Tal asking "Why Dor Shachar?" and Dor answering "Dor means generation. Shachar means dawn. A new day and a new life." Tal cut it, and the reason is structural rather than economic: Dor's actual final line is **"I chose life and not death. Love and not hatred. Light and not darkness"** — *light* is what *shachar* means, said by him, with nothing translated. Defining the name sixty seconds earlier spends the image before the ending can land it. It also stacked two statements of one idea back to back ("a new life" / "I chose life"), the same cancellation logged on the Social Accords script. **Before any glossing or translation beat, check whether the closing line already delivers that meaning as feeling — if it does, the gloss is the explaining line to delete.** Where the information is genuinely nice to have, it goes on screen as text (*Dor = generation · Shachar = dawn*), which costs zero seconds of audio.

**Cut for arithmetic:** the birth year (1977). Between "1977," "eleven" and "thirteen" the viewer is doing sums instead of listening. **Ages carry a story; birth years carry nothing.** Same instinct as cutting Dr Aki Tamir's name on Julius.

**The conversion section was the last thing fixed, and it was the thinnest.** For several drafts it read "For years he studied. He fought for his legal status. And he converted" — a summary of the hardest thing the subject ever did. Tal called it out. The rebuild does three things: states the obstacle first (*"you cannot just decide to be Jewish"*), makes the effort concrete and small (*"he had to learn to read"*), and finds a single physical image for the moment itself — the mikveh: *"He went into the water… and came out with a new name."* That image also hands the name beat its meaning, so the two blocks stopped competing. **When a transformation is summarised rather than shown, look for the one physical moment it actually happened in.**

**Same principle applied to the Gaza education section.** Tal asked for more on how the children around him were raised. The fix was not more narration about Gaza's school system — which would be a claim about a place — but more testimony from Dor about his own classroom: *"It wasn't just me. Every kid in my class learned the same thing."* **A subject describing his own childhood is testimony; a narrator describing a society is an argument. Always take the testimony.**

**NEW DEVICE — plant the consequence, then walk the hero into it.** Tal's addition: as a child Dor saw men hanged in the street in Gaza as suspected collaborators with Israel. It is stated once, early, in four lines, and never referred to again — but it detonates twice. First on the bus: an eleven-year-old who has watched that gets on a workers' bus into Israel anyway. Then in prison: *"they thought I was working for Israel"* lands as terror rather than misfortune, because the viewer already knows what that accusation means there. **The single most efficient thing a script can do is establish the stakes of an accusation long before the accusation arrives — then never mention them again.** Same plant-and-payoff mechanic as the three legs, used for dread instead of irony. Timeline checks out: the First Intifada began in December 1987, when Dor was about ten, and collaborator killings peaked across those years.

**A specific age beats a general upbringing.** "At school, at home, he was taught to hate Jews" is a condition. *"I was seven years old when my father told me that killing a Jew was the greatest thing you could do"* is a scene, with a man and a child in it. Tal asked for more on the Gaza education and the answer was not more scope — it was one age, one person, one sentence. **When a script needs to convey how someone was raised, find the youngest age at which something specific was said to them.**

**Cut on judgement, flagged rather than silently dropped:** Tal asked about mentioning that Jews used to live in Gaza before Oslo and the intifadas. Left out, with the reasoning given to him: there are two unrelated histories there — the old Jewish community of Gaza City, which ended in the 1929 riots almost fifty years before Dor was born, and Gush Katif, the settlements of his actual childhood, which is the most contested thing the video could touch. Either version converts a personal story into a political argument at its midpoint, and this script's whole power is that it never argues. The honest one-line version offered if he wants it: "There had been a Jewish community in Gaza for centuries. It ended in 1929."

**NEW STANDING RULE — an anecdote is a scene, and a scene belongs to one teller.** The logged split (VO carries chronology and fact, the subject carries feeling and decision) does not cover a subject telling a story with dialogue inside it. A draft of the candy beat ran: VO "He kept the wrapper and ran home to his father" → DOR "Dad, get me candy like this" → DOR "From Jews" → VO "His father told him never to take anything from them again." Tal's reaction: "that doesn't make sense, bro." He was right. Narration inside someone's story is an interruption, and a narrator finishing the subject's sentence strands the emotional payload in the wrong mouth. The fix is to hand the whole anecdote to the subject, unbroken, and let his own attributions carry it — "I said…" / "He said…" / "I told him…" / "He screamed at me…". **The same bug was present in three separate sections of the same script and is the most common structural error when assembling from testimony.**

**Corollary — the host's line lands after the scene, never inside it.** "He was six years old" originally sat between the class shouting "Slaughter the Jews" and Dor being slapped, breaking his story in half. Moved to after the slap, the same five words became the punch: you hear a child get hit, *then* you're told how old he was. Same move with "His name was Nissim," which was cut from the introduction and placed after the viewer has watched a stranger feed the kid every evening for weeks — naming him first makes him a character, naming him last makes him a person.

**NEW STANDING CHECK — when you cut an image, check what was leaning on it.** The mikveh was cut on Tal's instruction ("I don't know if we need the mikveh moment") — correctly, since a viewer who doesn't know what a mikveh is just sees a man getting into water. But the next line, *"and when it happened, all the pain disappeared,"* was left in place with nothing for "it" to refer to. Same orphaned-referent bug logged on the Dead Sea goat and the Boys Town robotics cut. **Removing a beat is not finished until every pronoun, "it," "that" and "the X" downstream has been re-checked.**

**The replacement was better than the thing removed, and the pattern is worth keeping: prefer the scene to the image.** Asked "did you mention how he became a Jew?", the honest answer was no — the script jumped from "it took me seven years" to the new name. The fix came from his own testimony: the rabbis asked what he would do if they refused him, and he answered **"If I am not Jewish, then I have no life."** A question and an answer beats a picture of a ritual, because it answers what the viewer actually wants to know — not what the ceremony looked like, but whether they were going to let him in. It also reframes the seven years from seven years of homework into seven years of being told no.

**Regression watch — a second writer's draft can undo a decision without knowing it was a decision.** Tal brought in an alternative version (ChatGPT-authored, complete with sourced footnotes) and asked whether it was better. One thing in it genuinely was: it restored the deportation, prison and torture, and placed "becoming Jewish would cost him almost everything" directly after the Seder — without which "it took me seven years" is administrative rather than earned. That was a real error on my side, caused by leaving the beat flagged as unresolved instead of going and verifying it. But the same draft silently reintroduced the name gloss at the close (cut an hour earlier for colliding with "light, and not darkness"), replaced "he was six years old" with "he was still just a child" (vague for specific, because sources disagree on the age — the fix is to ask the subject, not to blur it), downgraded "Toys" to "gifts," and kept the 27-years statistic while cutting both lines that gave it meaning. **The correct response to an outside draft is to name precisely what it does better, take only that, and say why each rejected change was a decision rather than an oversight.**

Accuracy handling: no Ayyash (West Bank, not Khan Yunis). Say "Palestinian security forces," never Hamas — Hamas did not govern Gaza until 2007. The torture, the seven months and the Sinwar claim are all in Dor's own mouth, which is where contested testimony belongs. Dor ran from his father; he was not abandoned (an earlier draft had this backwards and it was saved wrong into this library before Tal corrected it — **when a subject's biography is being assembled across many messages, re-verify any causal fact you inferred rather than were told**). The Sabar family calling him their brother is the only third-party-corroborated fact in the biography (Jewish Journal) and is therefore load-bearing for the ending. Interview prompts that produced the script's best material: **"What were you actually looking for that day?"**, **"What was the first thing Nissim said to you?"**, **"When did your father leave?"**, **"How long did it take you to convert?"**, **"Why Dor Shachar?"**

- **"I told him I ran away from my father"** (9026) resolved a line flagged as weak across four drafts. Neither "I don't have a father" nor "my father wasn't there" was as good as the truth.
- **"We ate together and we watched television"** (9029) replaced toys and clothes. Charity is a gift; television is an evening at home, which is the thing he had never had.
- **"They tied my legs up and my head down"** (9035) replaced "tortured me." A picture, not a category.

**NEW DEVICE — the body as evidence.** Tal's call: after "seven months I was in prison," Dor shows what they did to his arm. No VO, no music, three seconds of hold. It is the only moment in the video where the story stops being something he tells you. **Whenever a subject carries physical proof of what they describe, give it a silent beat of its own — narration over it turns evidence back into anecdote.** Followed immediately by his own "I want to live. I don't want to die" (9038), the raw version of the polished line he gives in published interviews.

**Scale through a number the subject supplies.** IMG_8997: **"there were fifty children in my school."** Fifty children stand up and shout; one feels sick and asks to leave. The script never says he was alone — the two numbers do it.

**A line was cut for being false, and Tal caught it.** A draft had TAL say "He had never met one" after the three-legs beat. It is contradicted thirty seconds later by the army base, where he played football with soldiers every day. **Check every host line against the rest of the cut, not only against the research** — and note it was also an explaining line, telling the viewer what the juxtaposition already showed. Removing it let three legs run straight into "but his father worked in Israel for 27 years," which is stronger.

**The parallel needed its second half.** "His son crossed it once and stayed" had nothing to be the opposite of. Tal's fix: **"His father crossed that border for 27 years… and always went home. His son decided never to go back."** Same border, opposite decision. A contrast line fails when only one side of it is stated.

**One word carried the hinge of the video:** "**But** one evening… a religious Jewish man walked over to him." He is sleeping on the street — *but* somebody came. Tal supplied the "but," and it is what turns the next beat from the next thing that happened into the thing that changed everything.

**NEW STANDING CHECK — a reversed generalisation is still a generalisation.** The shot ending had Dor say "the Jews are good… that's how the Jews are." Tal rejected it: "I don't really like that, it doesn't really make sense." The reason is structural — he was raised on "the Jews are X," and closing on "the Jews are Y" runs the same machinery pointed the other way. The fix keeps his evidence ("they gave me clothes and food"), cuts the category conclusion, and lets Tal state the smaller unarguable point: **"Nobody ever taught him to love Jews. One man just gave him dinner."** Not a program, not an education — one act any viewer could perform this week. Then the love is one person with a name: **"I love Nissim."** A named individual always beats a category.

**The message arrives once, at the end, and nowhere else.** Tal asked for the video to carry a message about children being taught to hate. Stating it early would have made everything after it an illustration of a thesis; held to the end it is a conclusion the viewer has already reached. Same architecture as the Social Accords manifesto — the lived experiment first, the finding last. Three lines carry it: *"Imagine being taught to hate a religion… and then becoming it"* (Tal's own line turned to second person so it addresses the viewer), the dinner line, and *"sometimes all it takes is meeting the person you were taught to hate."*

**NEW OUTRO TYPE — the platform ask.** "We have to fight every kind of hate. Together. I'm using my platform for that. Will you use yours?" Shot in two framings: the first line standing **with** the subject, the ask **alone to camera** — the framing change is the signal that the story is over and Tal is now talking to the viewer. Note the scope: *every kind of hate*, not antisemitism, which makes the ask available to everyone watching. Tal rejected "we're all human beings" as the closer (correctly — the stacked-abstraction problem logged on Social Accords); the spoken-language version that survived is **"you find out they're just people."** A trackable "comment the word PEACE" was available, since Dor plants the word in Arabic eight seconds earlier, and was deliberately left off: that device belongs to a recruitment video, not a story.

**Language handling.** Dor's Hebrew is fluent after thirty years; telling his childhood in **Arabic was a choice, not a limitation** — the man who became a Jew describing being taught to kill Jews, in the language they taught him in. Only two things are not in Arabic: the English hook ("I am a Jew living in Israel" — the contradiction is audible before a single fact is stated) and **the one Hebrew sentence, quoted inside an Arabic sentence**: "what came out of my mouth, in Hebrew, was — I want to be Jewish." That beat only exists because Nissim spoke Arabic and Hebrew came out; if he quotes himself in Arabic the beat collapses. **When a subject recounts speaking another language, the quoted phrase must stay in that language.**

**FINAL LOCKED CUT — 2:10, not 3:00.** Tal cut a third of the assembled edit script to hit runtime, and the triage order is the lesson. What went, in order: the mosque setup line (the sermon quote survives and carries it), the 46,000-workers statistic (the personal "27 years" does the same job), the third mention of 27 years — Tal's own best on-camera line, "his father crossed that border for 27 years and always went home; his son never did" — the father's death threat, the father's-permission irony, "imagine being taught to hate a religion and then becoming it," and the confirmed bus at eleven. **When a cut has to lose a third, the first things out are the second instance of any fact and the lines that state the theme; the last things out are the subject's own scenes.** Every surviving Dor clip is a scene; every cut Tal line was a statement.

**The one exception, and it is the rule worth keeping:** the three-legs plant was cut at 2:00 and restored at 2:10, because the payoff was rewritten to cost six words instead of forty. Instead of the bus (VO: he got on a bus at eleven, walked around Rishon LeZion, didn't find them — ~40 words), the payoff became a clause inside the base setup: **"And the soldiers who were supposed to have three legs… played football with him."** Same loop, one sixth of the runtime. **When a plant-and-payoff device is too expensive, do not cut the device — rewrite the payoff as a subordinate clause in a line you are already saying.**

Research corrections from the Jerusalem Post profile (jpost.com/israel-news/article-775358), obtained after the footage was mined: the three-legs claim and "Jews murder children, women, men and the elderly" came from the same teacher in the same lesson, at **age seven** — so the classroom is one beat, not two, and the on-camera "he was eleven years old" was wrong. The candy was at **six**, and the father's poison warning is confirmed. **The bus at eleven is real** — he boarded it without permission specifically to look for Jews with three legs, walked around Rishon LeZion, and went home that evening. He ran at **thirteen** and did not return to Gaza until **nineteen and a half**. The beating was administered by the **principal with a rubber hose**, though Dor's own footage says "the teacher hit me" and his words win. His father threatened to **hang or burn him** — which identifies the rope/gas/fire image that opens his written testimony and had been flagged as possibly a nightmare. The torture is **six months** in the JPost account and **seven** in his own footage; his number is used. The seven years was **waiting for state permission to study**, then ten months of yeshiva, then Machon Meir with Rabbi Oury Cherki. The second adoptive family is **Tzabar** (Gur and Amira), not Sabar. His book is **From Khan Yunis to Mount Sinai**. Shachar is glossed as **morning** in the JPost piece, dawn elsewhere.

Two sentences, no commentary, and it makes "becoming Jewish would cost him everything" concrete before the prison does. At 2:10 it costs twelve seconds. It is the first thing to restore if the runtime ever opens up.

**A subtitled cut runs 15–20% slower than its word count suggests.** Confirmed again here: 370 English-equivalent words landed near 2:05, not 1:57, because roughly 160 of them are read rather than heard. Budget for it before promising a runtime.

## Tal's line-level rewrite patterns (his own edits, before → after)

Captured verbatim from Tal's revisions. These are his ear, not rules derived from theory — when a line could go either way, this is the direction he takes it.

| Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "with no money for food" | "without enough money for food" | softer, less absolute, more true |
| "that was lunch" | "and sometimes… that was lunch" | the hedge makes it believable |
| "the neighbors" | "the neighbors who had even less" | the comparison is the whole point |
| "they were about to throw in the garbage" | "that sellers were about to throw away" | names who, drops the harsher noun |
| "eating cucumbers from the market garbage" | "cucumbers rescued from the market garbage" | "rescued" does the emotional work |
| "a daughter still wants to get married" | "life doesn't stop. A daughter still dreams of getting married." | state the principle, then the image |
| "the second-highest rate in the developed world" | "the second-highest child-poverty rate…" | name the metric or the stat floats |
| "four warehouses. Fifteen trucks. 95 distribution points." | "12,000 families get a food basket." | count people, not equipment |
| "17,200 packages" | "over 17,000 packages" | round for the ear |
| "In the north — 40% of them." | (cut) | one war statistic is enough |
| "the impact was even worse" then silence | "And they didn't stop." plus proof | never raise a problem and walk away |
| VO: "he started with 50 food baskets" | ARYEH: "I started with just 50 food baskets" | first person beats narration about him |
| "This is Israel. One of the richest countries in the Middle East." | (cut entirely) | don't establish a country; let it surface where it matters |
| "their country" | "this country" | the host is standing in it; stay consistent with the hook |

**The single most repeated instruction across every script: cut the line that explains what the previous line already showed.** Tal removes the summarizing beat almost every time — "that's the whole trick," "sometimes the person who understands the problem best is the person who lived through it," "every child, rich or poor, deserves the chance to dream." If an image has landed, the sentence interpreting it is the one to delete.

Update 2026-09-17: the hook stat was corrected from "one in three children is born into poverty" (income poverty in Israel is 28%, i.e. one in four) to "one in three children doesn't have enough food to eat" (Latet, 34% child food insecurity). The corrected line is also structurally better: it is answered later in the same script by the 3,000 free meals a day, so the opening statistic now pays off instead of sitting inert, and the closing line's "an education, food, and somebody who believes in them" reads as deliberate. See the Aryeh Lurie entry for the general lesson — when the user insists on a figure your first source contradicts, look for a different credible measure that makes the instinct true.
